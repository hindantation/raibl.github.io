Guess, Vogue, and the AI-generated model debate

The fashion brand and fashion magazine faced a consumer and industry backlash when using AI-generated images to promote clothing. Magazine readers felt misled as there wasn't an adequate disclosure that real models weren't used in the advertisement. Voices from the creative industry echoed the issue of replacing human talent with AI.

Incident type AI model non-disclosure, industry displacement
Date August 2025
Risk Disclosure failure, beauty standards, labour displacement, media trust

When fashion magazine Vogue's latest issue landed on newsstands in August 2025, it contained an advertisement for fashion brand Guess featuring two strikingly composed models Vivienne and Anastasia. Neither of them exists. Both were entirely AI-generated by Seraphinne Vallora, a London-based AI design studio whose synthetic models had already appeared in Elle, Grazia, Harper's Bazaar, the Financial Times, and the Wall Street Journal.

A disclosure did appear in the advertisement. In small print, a line read: “Produced by Seraphinne Vallora on AI.”, but many readers missed it. When the AI origins of the models were identified publicly, criticism was directed not only at Guess but also at the magazine. The backlash centred on two distinct issues: the adequacy of disclosure, and the displacement of creative labour. Photographers, casting agents, production teams, make-up artists, and real models whose roles the AI imagery had replaced.

What went wrong

The use of AI-generated models raises fundamental questions about how this technology is reshaping the creative industry. Who benefits from synthetic talent? Which body ideals are being amplified? How clearly are consumers informed that the people shown in an advertisement are not real? Fine-print wording that most readers are unlikely to notice does not amount to meaningful disclosure. In fashion and beauty, the use of synthetic bodies is not a neutral creative choice. It influences what audiences perceive as desirable, normal, and commercially valuable, while potentially displacing models, photographers, stylists, make-up artists, and other creative professionals. This incident therefore exposes a broader governance gap: responsibility for disclosure and representation risks is often fragmented across brands, agencies, AI providers, and publishers. The central question is not only whether disclosure was technically included, but who is responsible for ensuring that it is meaningful, visible and consistently enforced.

Marketing lesson: Disclosure is not enough if the underlying creative choice reinforces a harmful standard, or if the disclosure lacks sufficient visibility and is placed where consumers are unlikely to notice it.

Governance questions

  1. Does the brand have a policy for the use of synthetic people in advertising, and does it address representation risks?
  2. If your brand used AI-generated people in an advertisement, what would you include in the disclosure, and where would you place it?
  3. How would you included impacted creative workers, including models, photographers, stylists, and agencies, in the decision to use AI-generated imagery?

Learning outcomes

After discussing this case, participants should be able to:

  1. Explain the difference between technically present disclosure (fine print) and meaningful disclosure (consumer awareness).
  2. Identify who bears responsibility for AI disclosure in a multi-party creative arrangement: brand, agency, and publisher.
  3. Describe the specific categories of worker whose roles are affected, when AI-generated models replace real ones in commercial photography.
  4. Apply the principle of material disclosure: if knowing an image was AI-generated would change how a consumer views the brand, disclosure is not optional.
  5. Assess whether your organisation’s current AI creative policy covers the use of synthetic people in advertising materials, including stock imagery and campaign creative.

Discussion questions

  1. If your brand used AI-generated people in an ad, where would the disclosure appear and would your average consumer actually see it?
  2. Does your AI creative policy cover the use of AI-generated models, presenters, or influencers or only AI-generated copy and imagery?
  3. How would your brand respond if a journalist published a story identifying undisclosed AI-generated people in your advertising?
